How can 5 litres of energy drive a car 350 km?

Today you will build an electric car's floor in 3D, move lithium inside a battery cell, and work out what an EV really costs per km.

START WITH A PUZZLE

A Tata Nexon EV has a 45 kWh battery. That sounds big. But 1 litre of petrol holds about 9.5 kWh of energy. So 45 ÷ 9.5 = about 5 litres. The whole battery holds the energy of a small water bottle of petrol.

A petrol hatchback goes about 80 km on 5 litres. The Nexon EV goes about 350 km on its battery in real driving.

How can the EV go more than 4 times further on the same energy?

Guess first. Even a wrong guess helps you remember the answer.

CHAPTER 1

Where does the energy go?

Touch the bonnet of a petrol car after a drive. It is very hot. That heat is petrol you paid for.

On Day 4 you saw that an engine is a set of small fires. Fire makes heat first. Only a small part of that heat becomes push. The rest leaves through the exhaust and the radiator.

An electric motor does not burn anything. It uses magnets to turn electricity straight into turning. So very little is lost. Out of every 100 units of energy, look how many reach the wheels:

Green = reaches the wheels. Other colours = lost as heat. City driving, rough numbers.

CHAPTER 3

Inside one cell: lithium goes back and forth

Every cell you just built works the same way as the cell in your phone. Only bigger.

Think of two parking lots with a river between them. The left lot is graphite (like pencil lead). The right lot is a metal oxide. The cars are tiny lithium bits called ions.

When the battery is full, the ions are parked in the graphite. They want to go right. When you drive, they cross the river. But each ion leaves its electron behind. The electron cannot cross. It must go the long way, through the wire and through the motor. That flow of electrons is the current that turns your wheels.

Each part has a job:

  • Graphite: holds the ions when the battery is full. Without it: nowhere to store them.
  • Metal oxide: holds the ions when the battery is empty. Without it: the ions have no reason to move, so no energy.
  • Electrolyte: the liquid "river" that lets ions swim. Without it: ions are stuck and the cell is dead.
  • Separator: a plastic sheet full of tiny holes, thinner than a hair. Ions pass, electrons cannot. Without it: the two sides touch, short-circuit, and get very hot. This is how battery fires start.

Charging is the same trip backwards. The charger pushes electrons back into the graphite, and the ions follow them home.

One cell gives only about 3.7 volts, like your phone. The car needs about 350 volts. So the cells are joined in a chain: 96 × 3.7 = 355 V. That is why the orange wires in the 3D model are dangerous to touch.

CHAPTER 4

Why an EV jumps off the signal, and needs no gearbox

Remember Day 5? A petrol engine has a happy zone, about 1,000 to 6,000 rpm. Below that, it has almost no twist. So it needs a clutch and 5 gears.

An electric motor gives its full twist from 0 rpm. The moment you press the pedal, it pulls. It also spins happily past 12,000 rpm. So one fixed gear is enough. No clutch. No gear change.

Braking that fills the battery

On Day 2 you learned that brakes turn your speed into heat. Wasted. An EV does something smarter. When you lift your foot, the motor runs backwards as a generator. The wheels turn the motor, and the motor charges the battery. This is regenerative braking, or regen. Press "Brake (regen)" in the 3D model to see the flow turn around.

Energy of motion = ½ × mass × speed². Car with 2 people: about 1,700 kg. Some is lost in the motor, tyres and air, so roughly 60% comes back.

That is not free energy. It is getting back some of what you spent speeding up. In Bengaluru or Mumbai traffic, with stops every few hundred metres, it adds up. Brake pads on EVs last much longer too.

CHAPTER 5

Charging: the bucket and the tap

Two words confuse everyone: kWh and kW. Think of a bucket and a tap.

  • kWh is the bucket. How much energy the battery holds. The Nexon EV bucket is 45 kWh.
  • kW is the tap. How fast energy flows in. A home plug is a thin tap. A fast charger is a fire hose.

Time = bucket to fill ÷ tap speed. Pick a charger and how full you want it:

Why fast charging slows down after 80%

Think of filling a bus. When it is empty, people rush in. When it is nearly full, the last people must squeeze in slowly to find a seat. Ions are the same. Near full, the graphite has few free spots. Pushing hard then makes heat and can damage the cell. So the car's brain slows the charging down.

Battery life in India: a battery ages faster when it is hot, kept at 100% for long, or fast-charged every single day. Charge to about 80 to 90% for daily use. Park in shade in summer. Go to 100% just before a long trip.

TRUE OR FALSE?

Three things people believe

"An EV battery dies in 3 or 4 years, and a new one costs as much as the car."

False. Car batteries lose capacity slowly, a little each year. The BMS and the cooling plate protect them far better than a phone protects its battery. Most makers in India give a warranty of around 8 years. Check the exact terms for any car you buy.

"Driving an EV on a waterlogged road will give you an electric shock."

False. The battery case is sealed against water and dust (many are rated IP67: underwater at 1 metre for 30 minutes). The high-voltage system is kept apart from the car body, and the BMS cuts power if it senses a fault. But deep water can still damage any car, petrol or electric. Avoid it if you can.

"Charge to 100% every night to keep the battery healthy."

False. A cell sitting full for hours ages faster, especially in heat. For daily use, 80 to 90% is gentler. Your phone's "optimised charging" setting does the same trick. (Some LFP batteries are an exception; follow the car's manual.)

RECALL QUIZ

No scrolling up. Answer in your head first.

1. Why does an EV go so much further on the same energy?

A petrol engine turns most of its energy into heat. Only about 20% reaches the wheels. An electric motor and inverter waste very little, so about 85 to 90% reaches the wheels. Regen braking also gets some back.

2. What does the BMS do?

It is the battery's brain. It watches the voltage and temperature of every cell, balances them, controls charging speed, and cuts power if something is wrong.

3. kWh or kW: which one tells you how long a charge takes?

You need both. kWh is the size of the bucket. kW is the speed of the tap. Time = kWh to add ÷ kW. For example, 31.5 kWh ÷ 6 kW = about 5 hours.

Spaced review from yesterday (Day 5, Gearbox and clutch):

4. A small gear with 12 teeth turns a big gear with 42 teeth. What do you gain and lose?

Ratio 42 ÷ 12 = 3.5. The big gear twists 3.5 times harder, but turns 3.5 times slower.

5. Why is holding a car on a slope at the clutch's bite point a bad habit?

The plates slip the whole time. The engine's work turns into heat in the clutch, and it wears out fast. Use the brake or handbrake instead.

  • kWh: amount of energy (the bucket). 1 kWh = 1 unit on your bill. kW: rate of flow (the tap).
  • SoC (state of charge): how full the battery is, in %.
  • Inverter: turns the battery's DC into 3-phase AC for the motor, and controls its speed. Runs backwards during regen.
  • Onboard charger: turns AC from your home into DC for the battery. A DC fast charger skips it and feeds the battery directly, which is why it is faster.
  • Connectors in India: cars mostly use Type 2 for AC and CCS2 for DC fast charging.
  • Chemistries: NMC (nickel manganese cobalt) packs more energy in less weight. LFP (lithium iron phosphate) is cheaper, safer with heat, and lasts more cycles, but is heavier for the same range.
  • Cell shapes: cylinder (like a big AA), prismatic (flat hard box), pouch (soft flat packet).
  • Orange cables on any EV carry high voltage. Never cut or touch them. Rescue teams are trained to look for them.
  • Range drops with: high speed (air drag grows with speed²), AC on full, heavy load, steep hills, hard acceleration.
