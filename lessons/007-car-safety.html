Why is a car that crumples safer?

Today you will crash a car in slow motion, race an airbag against a blink, brake with and without ABS, and learn to read a crash test star rating. Then you take your first weekly exam.

START WITH A PUZZLE

Two cars hit a wall at 64 km/h. Car A is an old, very stiff car. Its front only gets a small dent. Car B is a new car. Its front folds up like a paper fan, about 60 cm shorter.

Uncle says: "See? Old cars were made of real steel. New cars are like tin."

Which car would you rather sit in?

Guess first. Even a wrong guess helps you remember the answer.

CHAPTER 1

It is not the speed that hurts. It is the sudden stop.

Jump off a wall onto a hard floor with straight legs. Ouch. Now jump again and bend your knees. Same height. Same speed. Much less pain. Why?

Bending your knees makes you stop over a longer distance. A longer stop means a gentler stop. Your body feels less force.

A crash works the same way. At 64 km/h, your body has the same energy as falling from a 5-floor building. (64 km/h = 17.8 m/s. Height = 17.8 × 17.8 ÷ (2 × 9.8) = 16 m.) You cannot remove that energy. You can only make the stop longer and softer.

Three things stretch your stop. Turn them on and off and watch the force on a 70 kg person:

Average force = speed² ÷ (2 × stopping distance). "1 g" is your normal weight. 16 g means you feel 16 times heavier for a moment. Rough numbers: real crashes have short peaks above the average.

So the answer to the puzzle: Car B. Its folding front is the bent knees. The stiff car stops in a few centimetres, and your body takes the hit instead of the metal. Metal is cheap. You are not.

Quick check: turn the seatbelt OFF. Why does the crumple zone stop helping?

Without a belt, you are not tied to the car. The car slows down gently, but you keep flying at full speed. Then you hit the steering wheel or glass, which has already stopped. Your stop is only a few centimetres long. The belt is what lets you "ride down" with the car.

CHAPTER 3

The airbag race: faster than a blink

A blink takes about 100 to 150 milliseconds. (1 millisecond = one thousandth of a second.) The whole crash is over in about the same time. So a car cannot wait for you. Everything must already be decided.

Slide through the crash. This is the same slider as the 3D car above.

The jobs, and why each exists

  • Crash sensors and airbag brain: measure how fast the car is slowing down, about 1,000 times a second. Without them: the airbag would fire at a pothole, or not at all.
  • Seatbelt: ties you to the car, across the strong bones of your hips and chest. Without it: you fly forward at full speed, as you saw in Chapter 1.
  • Pretensioner: a small charge pulls the belt tight in the first 15 ms, removing slack from a loose jacket. Without it: you move forward several centimetres before the belt even starts to hold.
  • Load limiter: lets the belt give out a little once the force gets very high. Without it: the belt itself could break ribs.
  • Airbag: a nylon bag filled by a fast chemical reaction that makes harmless gas. It opens faster than 200 km/h, then lets gas out through holes so you sink in softly. Without it: your head swings forward and hits the steering wheel.

Your airbag says SRS on it. That means "Supplementary Restraint System". Supplementary means "extra". The airbag is built to work with a belt. An unbelted person can hit the bag while it is still opening, at more than 200 km/h. That is why the car beeps until you buckle up.

The back seat is not safe without a belt

A 70 kg passenger in the back, with no belt, at 50 km/h. They fly forward and hit the front seat. They stop in about 10 cm. 13.9 × 13.9 ÷ (2 × 0.1) = 965 m/s², about 98 g. 70 kg × 98 = about 6,900 kg of force. That is heavier than an adult elephant, pressing on the person in front of them.

In 2022, businessman Cyrus Mistry died in a crash on a highway near Mumbai. He was in the back seat without a belt. After this, India moved to make rear seat belt reminders compulsory in new cars.

CHAPTER 4

ABS: brake hard and still steer

The safest crash is the one that never happens. So now we move from surviving a crash to avoiding one.

Remember Day 1? A tyre grips best when it is rolling. Once it stops turning and slides, grip drops. And on Day 2, you learned that hard braking can lock the wheels.

A locked wheel has one more problem. A sliding tyre cannot steer. You turn the steering wheel, and the car keeps going straight, like a slab of soap on a wet floor.

ABS (Anti-lock Braking System) watches each wheel with a speed sensor. When a wheel is about to lock, it lets the brake go for a split second, then squeezes again. It does this about 15 times a second. You feel it as a buzzing in the pedal.

Try it. An auto-rickshaw stops suddenly 30 m ahead. You slam the brake at once.

Distance = speed² ÷ (2 × grip × 9.8), the Day 2 formula. Grip numbers are typical for good tyres: dry 0.9 rolling, 0.7 sliding; wet 0.55 rolling, 0.4 sliding. We ignore your reaction time here (Day 2 added about 1 second).

The big win is steering, not distance. On a good road, ABS often stops a little shorter. On loose gravel or sand, it can even stop a little longer. But you keep control, so you can steer around the problem.

How to brake in an emergency with ABS: press the pedal hard, keep it pressed, and steer. Do not pump it. Do not be scared of the buzzing. ABS is fitted on every new car sold in India since 2019.

ESC (Electronic Stability Control) is ABS's bigger brother. It uses the same brake unit. If the car starts to spin in a fast turn, it brakes one single wheel to pull the car straight. Without it: a sudden swerve at highway speed can spin the car.

CHAPTER 5

Crash tests: how a car earns its stars

You cannot crash-test a car before buying it. So someone does it for you. In India, that is Bharat NCAP (New Car Assessment Programme), run by the government since late 2023. Before that, Indian cars were tested by Global NCAP, a charity.

Testers buy cars, put crash dummies full of sensors inside, and crash them. Three main tests:

  • Front offset crash, 64 km/h: the driver's side of the front hits a soft aluminium block. Only part of the front takes the hit, so one crash rail must do most of the work. This is the most common serious crash on a road.
  • Side crash, 50 km/h: a heavy trolley hits the driver's door. There is almost no crumple zone in a door, so the cage and side airbags matter most.
  • Side pole crash, 29 km/h: the car slides sideways into a pole, like a tree. Only for cars that already score 3 stars or more.

The car gets two scores. Adult occupant protection is out of 32. Child occupant protection is out of 49, using child dummies in child seats. Each turns into stars. Try it:

A score is not only about the dummy. To get 3 stars or more, the car must also have ESC and seatbelt reminders. A car with great metal but missing these cannot get a high rating.

From October 2027, the government plans "Bharat NCAP 2.0". It will also score how well a car protects people walking outside, how well it avoids crashes (like automatic emergency braking), and how easy it is to rescue people after a crash. So a 5-star car of today may score lower under the new rules.

TRUE OR FALSE?

Three things people believe

"My car has 6 airbags, so I do not need to wear a seatbelt."

False. The belt does most of the work. Airbags are "supplementary", built to work with a belt. Without one, you can hit the bag while it is still opening, which can injure you badly.

"Old cars were heavier and stiffer, so they were safer."

False. A stiff front stops in a few centimetres, so the force goes into your body. Good cars have a soft front that crumples and a hard cabin that does not. Less damage to the car can mean more damage to you.

"With ABS, you should pump the brake pedal in an emergency."

False. Pumping was the old trick for cars without ABS. ABS pumps for you, about 15 times a second, much faster than your foot. Press hard, hold, and steer.

RECALL QUIZ

No scrolling up. Answer in your head first.

1. Why is a crumple zone good, even though it wrecks the car?

It makes the stop longer. A longer stop means a smaller force on your body. The crushed metal soaks up the energy instead of you, while the hard cabin keeps its shape.

2. Why does an airbag need a seatbelt?

The belt holds you in the right place and slows you with the car. The airbag only cushions your head and chest at the end. Without a belt, you can hit the bag while it is still opening, very fast.

3. What is the main gift of ABS?

You can still steer while braking hard. It stops the wheels from locking, by releasing and squeezing the brakes about 15 times a second.

4. Inside a battery cell, what does the separator let through, and what does it block?

It lets lithium ions pass through its tiny holes. It blocks electrons, so they must go the long way through the wire and motor. If it fails, the cell short-circuits and gets very hot.

5. Why does a fast charger slow down after about 80%?

The graphite has few free spots left, like a nearly full bus. Pushing hard then makes heat and damages the cell, so the battery's brain (BMS) slows the charging.

WEEKLY EXAM: WEEK 1, CARS

Seven questions, one from each day

Say your answer out loud first. Then open it, and be honest: tap "Got it" or "Missed it". Your score is saved in this browser.

1. (Tyres) A tyre has too little air. Which part of the tread wears out fastest, and why?

The two edges. With less air, the tyre squashes more, the middle lifts a little, and the edges carry the weight.

2. (Brakes) You go from 40 to 80 km/h. How much longer is your braking distance?

4 times longer. Braking distance grows with speed times speed: 2 × 2 = 4.

3. (Suspension) Your car keeps bouncing after a speed breaker. Which part is worn out?

The shock absorber (damper). The spring holds the car up. The damper stops the bouncing by pushing oil through small holes.

4. (Engine) Name the four strokes. Which one pushes the car?

Suck (intake), squeeze (compression), bang (power), blow (exhaust). Only the bang pushes the piston.

5. (Gearbox) Why do you start in 1st gear and not 5th?

1st gear multiplies the engine's twist many times, so it can get a heavy, still car moving. 5th gear gives speed but very little twist, so the engine would stall.

6. (EVs) How does an EV get energy back when it slows down?

Regenerative braking. The wheels turn the motor like a generator, and it charges the battery. A petrol car's brakes turn that energy into heat instead.

7. (Safety) Join three days: why does a locked wheel make a crash more likely?

A sliding tyre grips less than a rolling one (Day 1), so you stop later (Day 2). And a sliding tyre cannot steer, so you cannot go around the danger. ABS prevents this (Day 7).
