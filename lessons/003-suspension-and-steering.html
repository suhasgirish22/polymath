Why doesn't your car bounce like a ball?

Today you will drive over a speed breaker, build a front wheel corner in 3D, and find out how a small steering wheel turns two big tyres.

START WITH A PUZZLE

You drive over a speed breaker at 20 km/h. In half a second, the wheel is pushed up 10 cm. Each wheel has a big spring. Springs bounce, like a ball.

But a good car goes up once and settles. It does not bounce again and again. What stops the bounce?

Guess first. Even a wrong guess helps you remember the answer.

CHAPTER 1

The spring carries the car

Stand on a mattress. It sinks a little, then holds you. The car's springs do the same job. They hold the body of the car up.

When a wheel hits a bump, the spring squashes. The wheel goes up. The body hardly moves. Without springs: every stone on the road would hit your seat directly, and the wheels would jump off the road.

A normal car spring needs about 2.4 kg to squash 1 mm. With 4 people inside, each spring holds about 310 kg. 310 ÷ 2.4 = about 130 mm. So the car sinks about 13 cm onto its springs. Add people and watch:

Bounces per second = 1 ÷ (2π) × √(spring stiffness ÷ weight). Car makers aim for about 1 to 1.5. That is close to the rhythm of your steps when you walk, so it feels natural.

CHAPTER 2

The shock absorber stops the bounce

Push a spoon slowly through honey. Easy. Now push it fast. Hard. Thick liquid fights fast movement. That is the trick inside a shock absorber (also called a damper).

It is a tube of oil with a piston inside. When the wheel moves, oil is forced through tiny holes. The oil gets warm. The bounce energy becomes heat, just like your brakes on Day 2.

Without it: the car bounces 5 or 6 times after every bump. On a bumpy road the tyre hops. A hopping tyre cannot brake or steer.

This is a real spring and damper model, worked out 500 times per second: spring force = stiffness × squash, damper force = strength × speed. 100% is "critical": the body never overshoots at all. Most cars sit near 25 to 40%.

Look closely while it plays. The spring and shock absorber get shorter together. When you steer, the whole pillar turns with the wheel. The rubber and bearing at the top let it spin.

CHAPTER 4

How a small wheel turns two big tyres

Your steering wheel turns a small gear called the pinion. Its teeth sit on a straight bar with teeth, called the rack. When the gear turns, the bar slides left or right. Tie rods carry that slide to the wheels.

It is a slow gear on purpose. You turn the steering wheel about 15° to turn the tyres 1°. That makes steering light and calm. Without that gear: a tiny shake of your hand would swing the car.

Now a surprise. In a turn, the two front wheels do not point the same way. The inside wheel runs on a smaller circle, so it must turn more. Turn the wheel and watch:

Today almost every new car has electric power steering. A small motor on the column or rack adds push, so parking is easy. It helps more at low speed and less at high speed.

CHAPTER 5

Why your car pulls to one side

The wheels must point in exactly the right direction. Mechanics call this alignment. A hard pothole hit can bend it a little. You cannot see it. But your tyres feel it.

The most important setting is toe: do the front tyres point straight ahead, or a little inward or outward? Even a tiny angle drags the tyre sideways while you drive, like pushing a shopping trolley with one stuck wheel.

Toe is measured as a gap in mm at the edge of the wheel. Try it:

Two other settings matter. Camber is the top of the tyre leaning in or out. If it is wrong, one edge of the tyre wears out first. Caster is why your steering wheel comes back to centre by itself after a turn. Look at a shopping trolley wheel: its pivot is in front of the wheel, so the wheel trails behind and points straight. Your front wheels are set up in a similar way.

TRUE OR FALSE?

Three things people believe

"Shock absorbers hold up the weight of the car."

False. The springs hold the weight. Shock absorbers only slow the movement down. A car with dead shocks stands at the same height. It just keeps bouncing.

"Stiffer suspension always gives better grip."

False. On a smooth race track, stiff helps in corners. On a broken Indian road, a very stiff car makes the tyres skip over bumps. A tyre in the air has zero grip.

"If the car pulls to one side, you need wheel balancing."

False. Pulling comes from alignment or uneven tyre pressure. Balancing fixes a shake in the steering at higher speed. Many shops do both together, which is why people mix them up.

RECALL QUIZ

No scrolling up. Answer in your head first.

1. Which part holds up the car, and which part stops the bouncing?

The spring holds the weight. The shock absorber (damper) stops the bouncing by pushing oil through small holes, turning the movement into heat.

2. When you turn left, which front wheel turns more, and why?

The left (inside) wheel. It runs on a smaller circle, so it must point more sharply into the turn.

3. What does the rack and pinion do?

It turns the spinning of your steering wheel into a sliding push left or right. Tie rods carry that push to the wheels. It is a slow gear, about 15 to 1.

Spaced review from yesterday (Day 2, Brakes):

4. Why should you use a low gear going down a long ghat?

The engine then resists the wheels and takes part of the work. The brakes stay cooler, so they do not fade and the fluid does not boil.

5. Why does an air bubble in brake pipes make the pedal soft?

Air can be squashed, liquid cannot. Your push squashes the bubble first, so the pedal goes down too far before the pads squeeze.

  • Spring: holds weight. Damper: controls speed of movement. Bushes: rubber pieces at every joint that stop noise and shake.
  • Anti-roll bar: a bent steel bar linking left and right wheels. It reduces body lean in corners.
  • Ground clearance: the gap under the car. Indian hatchbacks are often around 160 to 170 mm because of speed breakers.
  • Toe: tyres pointing in or out. Camber: tyre leaning in or out. Caster: tilt of the steering pivot that self-centres the steering.
  • Alignment: after a hard pothole, new tyres, or any suspension repair. Many people do it every 10,000 km.
  • Shock absorbers: wear slowly, so you may not notice. Many need replacing somewhere between 50,000 and 100,000 km. Use the bounce test.
