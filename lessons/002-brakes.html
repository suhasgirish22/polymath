Where does your speed go when you brake?

Today you will build a disc brake part by part, squeeze it, and see why it gets hot enough to make chai.

START WITH A PUZZLE

You are driving at 60 km/h. A dog runs onto the road. You press the brake. In about 2 seconds, your 1,400 kg car stands still.

The car had a lot of movement energy. Energy cannot just disappear. So where did it go?

Guess first. Even a wrong guess helps you remember the answer.

CHAPTER 1

A brake is a heat maker

Rub your palms together fast for 5 seconds. They get warm. You turned movement into heat. That is called friction.

A brake does the same thing. It rubs a pad against a spinning metal disc. The car slows down. The disc gets hot.

How much heat? Movement energy = half × mass × speed × speed. At 100 km/h (27.8 m/s): 0.5 × 1,400 × 27.8 × 27.8 = about 540,000 joules. That is enough to heat 1.7 litres of water from tap to boiling. One stop. Move the speed and see:

Disc rise assumes the two front discs (7 kg of iron each) take 70% of the heat. The front does most of the work, because the car's weight tips forward when you brake.

CHAPTER 2

How a small push becomes a huge squeeze

To stop fast, the pads must squeeze the disc with about the weight of a car. But your foot pushes maybe 20 kg. How?

Three tricks multiply your push, one after another:

  • The pedal is a lever. Long arm for your foot, short arm at the other end. It multiplies about 4 times.
  • The booster is the big round drum behind the pedal. It uses engine suction to add about 4 times more.
    Without it: you would have to stand on the pedal.
  • The liquid. A small piston pushes on brake fluid. The fluid pushes a big piston at the wheel. The push spreads over a bigger area, so the force grows.
Fluid pressure
82 bar
Squeeze on the disc
1,930 kg

The numbers: the small piston has 3.8 cm² of area. The big one has 22.9 cm². That is 6 times bigger, so the force is 6 times bigger. The same push reaches every wheel at once.

Quick check: why must there be no air in brake pipes?

Liquid cannot be squashed. Air can. With an air bubble, your push squashes the bubble first. The pedal feels soft and goes down too far. Mechanics call this a spongy pedal. It is dangerous.

CHAPTER 4

Why stopping takes longer than you think

Stopping has two parts. First your brain sees the danger and moves your foot. That takes about 1.5 seconds. The car does not slow at all in that time.

Then the brakes work. Here is the surprise: braking distance grows with speed × speed. Go 2 times faster, and you need 4 times the road to stop.

Try it. Change speed, road and your attention:

Braking distance = speed × speed ÷ (2 × grip × 9.81). Grip values are rough: dry 0.8, wet 0.55, wet with worn tyres 0.35. Remember Day 1: the tyre is what finally stops the car.

CHAPTER 5

Why brakes fail on long ghat roads

Think of the drive down a long ghat, like Khandala on the Mumbai to Pune road. You go down hundreds of metres. Gravity keeps adding speed. If only the brakes hold you back, all that energy becomes heat in the brakes.

Too much heat causes brake fade. Two things go wrong:

  • Hot pads grip less. You press the same, but the car slows less.
  • The fluid can boil. Brake fluid slowly soaks up water from the air. Old fluid boils at a lower temperature. Boiling makes gas bubbles, and you get the spongy pedal from Chapter 2.

The fix: use a low gear. The engine then resists the wheels and takes part of the work. This is engine braking. Pick a hill and a gear:

Energy from the hill
6.9 MJ
Same as hard stops from 100 km/h
11

Hill energy = mass × 9.81 × height. How much the gear takes is a rough idea. It depends on the car and speed.

Simple rule: go down a hill in the same gear you would use to climb it. Use short, firm presses. Do not ride the brake lightly all the way down.

TRUE OR FALSE?

Three things people believe

"In an emergency, pump the brake pedal again and again."

False for modern cars. Every new car in India has ABS. Press hard and keep pressing. ABS does the pumping for you, many times a second. You will learn how on Day 7.

"Brakes squeaking on a rainy morning means they are failing."

Usually false. A thin layer of rust forms on the disc overnight. The first few stops wipe it off. But a high squeal every time you brake can be the wear warning tab on a thin pad.

"Going downhill in neutral saves fuel."

False for modern cars. In gear with your foot off the pedal, the engine computer cuts fuel to almost zero. In neutral, the engine burns fuel to idle. And your brakes get much hotter.

RECALL QUIZ

No scrolling up. Answer in your head first.

1. When you brake, where does the car's movement energy go?

It turns into heat, mostly in the discs and pads. Then the heat slowly goes into the air.

2. Name the three things that multiply your foot's push.

The pedal lever (about 4 times), the booster (about 4 times more), and the fluid pushing a small piston into a bigger one (about 6 times more).

3. You double your speed. What happens to braking distance?

It becomes 4 times longer, because it grows with speed times speed.

Spaced review from yesterday (Day 1, Tyres):

4. At 33 psi, how big is the patch of tyre touching the road?

About 150 cm², the size of a postcard. 350 kg per wheel ÷ 2.3 kg per cm².

5. Why does a worn tyre make wet braking so much longer?

Shallow grooves cannot drain water. Less rubber touches the road, so grip drops. You saw it in Chapter 4: wet with worn tyres needs over twice the dry distance.

HOW IT CONNECTS
  • Physics: energy is never lost, only changed. Brakes change movement into heat. This rule shows up in every lesson about machines.
  • Same trick, other places: the jack at the tyre shop that lifts your car uses the same small piston and big piston idea.
  • Day 6, EVs: an electric car brakes partly with its motor. It turns movement back into battery charge instead of heat.
  • Tomorrow, suspension and steering: brakes only work while the tyre is pressed on the road. Over a bump, suspension keeps it there.

  • Disc brake: pads squeeze a disc. Cools well, works well when wet. Front of almost all cars.
  • Drum brake: curved shoes push outward inside a metal drum. Cheaper, and holds the handbrake easily. Rear of many Indian hatchbacks.
  • Pads: about 10 to 12 mm thick when new. Replace near 3 mm. Many pads have a metal tab that squeals when thin.
  • Discs: wear too. The minimum thickness is stamped on the disc (look for "MIN TH").
  • Brake fluid soaks up water from the air. That is why it must be changed, often every 2 years. Check your car's manual.
  • Safe gap: keep at least 3 seconds behind the car in front. Double it in rain.
