How does air hold up a car?

Today you will open up a tyre, build one layer by layer, and learn to read the one on your own car.

START WITH A PUZZLE

Your car weighs about 1,400 kg. The air in each tyre is at about 33 psi. That is only about twice the pressure inside a cooker when it whistles.

So how can soft air hold up a whole car?

Guess first. Even a wrong guess helps you remember the answer.

CHAPTER 1

The tyre squashes until it balances

The air pushes on every bit of the tyre from inside. At 33 psi, it pushes about 2.3 kg on every square centimetre.

The car pushes down. The bottom of the tyre goes flat. It keeps going flatter until the flat part is big enough to hold the weight.

One wheel carries about 350 kg. 350 ÷ 2.3 = about 150 cm². That is about the size of a postcard. Your whole car stands on four postcards of rubber.

Quick check: your tyre looks flat at the bottom. What happened?

It has less air. Less push per cm² means the tyre must squash more to hold the same weight. A squashed tyre bends more, gets hot and wears at the edges.

Your car's correct pressure is on a sticker inside the driver door frame. Not on the tyre.

CHAPTER 3

Why rubber grips the road

Rub a pencil eraser on paper. It grabs. Rub a plastic ruler. It slides. Why?

Rubber is soft. It sinks into the tiny bumps of the road and holds on, like your fingertip pressing into rough cloth. Hard plastic just sits on top of the bumps.

So softer rubber grips more. But there is a catch. Soft rubber also wears out faster. And every time it squashes and springs back, it turns some energy into heat. That lost energy is paid for with fuel.

CHAPTER 4

What happens in the rain

Think of the grooves as drains. When you drive on a wet road, the grooves push the water out, so the rubber can still touch the road.

A new car tyre has grooves about 8 mm deep. As you drive, they wear down. At 1.6 mm the tyre is finished. Small raised bars inside the grooves show you when you reach it.

If the grooves are too shallow, or you go too fast, the water cannot escape. It builds up in front and lifts the tyre. Now you are driving on water. Switch tyres and watch:

This is called aquaplaning. For those few seconds, the steering and brakes do nothing.

Monsoon tip: wet braking gets much worse long before 1.6 mm. If you drive a lot in heavy rain, many experts say change tyres at about 3 mm.

TRUE OR FALSE?

Three things people believe

"Fill air up to the number printed on the tyre."

False. That number is the most the tyre can safely hold. Use the number on your car's door sticker.

"Filling nitrogen makes a big difference."

Mostly false. Normal air is already about 78% nitrogen. Pure nitrogen leaks a little slower, but checking pressure every two weeks matters much more.

"Bigger alloy wheels with thin tyres give a softer ride."

False. A thin sidewall has less rubber to soak up bumps. The ride gets harsher, and the wheel bends more easily in potholes.

RECALL QUIZ

No scrolling up. Answer in your head first.

1. What really carries the weight of the car?

The air. The tyre squashes until the flat patch is big enough: pressure times patch area equals the weight on that wheel.

2. Which layer stops the tyre from blowing up like a balloon?

The body ply: fabric cords that run from one bead, over the top, to the other bead.

3. Why does a worn tyre aquaplane more easily?

Its grooves are shallow, so they cannot drain the water fast enough. The water lifts the tyre off the road.

HOW IT CONNECTS
  • Physics: the same pressure and area idea is why a camel's wide feet do not sink in sand.
  • Chemistry: in 1839 Charles Goodyear found that heating rubber with sulfur makes it strong and springy. This is called vulcanisation.
  • India: most of India's natural rubber grows in Kerala. MRF started in Chennai in 1946, making toy balloons.
  • Tomorrow, brakes: brakes stop the wheel. Tyres stop the car. A great brake on a bald tyre still skids.
