How do tiny fires push your car forward?

Today you will build a petrol engine in 3D, run it in slow motion, and find out where your petrol money really goes.

START WITH A PUZZLE

You fill Rs 1,000 of petrol. The engine burns all of it. Some of that energy pushes the car down the road. The rest is lost.

How much of your Rs 1,000 actually moves the car?

Guess first. Even a wrong guess helps you remember the answer.

CHAPTER 1

Fire inside a closed can

Light a match in open air. The hot gas just floats away. Now light it inside a closed steel can with one wall that can slide. The hot gas has nowhere to go. It pushes the sliding wall hard.

That is an engine. The can is the cylinder. The sliding wall is the piston. The fire is petrol mixed with air.

How hard does it push? A piston in a normal 1.2 litre Indian hatchback is 73 mm wide. Its top has an area of about 42 cm², the size of a large coin box lid. Each bar of pressure pushes about 1 kg on every cm². Move the slider:

The fuel for one bang is tiny. At full power, one cylinder takes in about 0.3 litre of air. It needs only about 1/40 of a gram of petrol. That is about the weight of one grain of rice.

But petrol is packed with energy. The energy in 1 litre could, in theory, lift your car about 3 km straight up. That is higher than Ooty.

CHAPTER 2

Suck, squeeze, bang, blow

One push is not enough. The engine must do it again and again, thousands of times a minute. So it repeats four steps, called strokes. A stroke is one trip of the piston, top to bottom or bottom to top.

Tap each stroke, or press Play. Watch the two little doors at the top. These are the valves. Blue lets air in. Grey lets smoke out.

Only one stroke out of four makes power: the bang. The other three use up energy. So what keeps the crank turning between bangs? A heavy spinning disc on the crank, called the flywheel, like a potter's wheel that keeps going after you push it. More cylinders also help: in a 4 cylinder engine, one of them is always on its bang.

The pressures come from real gas rules: squeezing to 1/11 of the space raises pressure about 22 times. Burning then multiplies it about 2.7 times more. Real engines spark a little before the top, but the idea is the same.

CHAPTER 4

Why squeeze before the bang?

Squeezing uses up energy. So why not skip it? Think of a catapult. Pull the rubber back further, and the stone flies further. The squeeze is the pull back.

When the mix is squeezed, the fire starts in a tiny, hot space. The gas then has a long way to grow as the piston goes down. Longer growing means more push from the same drop of petrol.

Engineers call this the compression ratio. 11 means the space at the bottom is 11 times the space at the top. Change it:

So why not squeeze to 20? Because hot squeezed petrol can explode by itself, before the spark, or in the wrong place. You hear it as a metal "pinging" on a hill. This is called knock. It can crack a piston. Without a limit: the engine would hammer itself to pieces.

The octane number tells you how well petrol resists knock. Normal petrol in India is 91 octane. Premium is 95 or more. Since 2025, most Indian pumps sell E20: petrol with 20% ethanol made from sugarcane and grain. Ethanol resists knock well, but one litre carries less energy, so mileage can drop a little in older cars.

The "best possible" number uses the ideal engine formula: 1 − 1 ÷ ratio0.4. Real engines lose heat and friction too, so the best car engines today reach about 35 to 40%.

CHAPTER 5

Where your petrol money goes

Back to the puzzle. Every bang makes the gas very hot. Most of that heat cannot be turned into push. It leaves through the exhaust pipe, or through the radiator. Pick how you drive:

    Rough split. The US Department of Energy says only about 12 to 30% of the fuel energy moves a normal petrol car.

    All that waste heat must leave, or the engine melts. Two helpers keep it alive:

    • Coolant and radiator: a water mix flows around the cylinder and carries heat to the radiator at the front. The fan blows air through it. Without it: in a few minutes the metal swells, the head bends and the engine seizes.
    • Engine oil: a thin film between every moving metal part. The piston slides on it. The crank spins on it. Without it: metal rubs on metal, gets red hot and welds itself together. This can kill an engine in under a minute.

    Day 2 link: the same loss is why "engine braking" works. Lift your foot and the engine still has to suck, squeeze and blow. That work slows the car, and your brakes stay cool on a ghat.

    TRUE OR FALSE?

    Three things people believe

    "Premium high-octane petrol gives every car more power."

    False. Octane is not extra energy. It only means the petrol resists knock better. If your manual says 91 octane, a normal engine will not get faster on 95. Engines tuned for high octane (some turbo cars) do benefit.

    "Warm up the engine for 10 minutes before driving."

    False for modern fuel-injected cars. About 30 seconds is enough, then drive gently for the first few km. Long idling wastes petrol and warms the engine slower than driving.

    "Change the engine oil only when it turns black."

    False. Oil has cleaners in it that hold dirt, so it goes dark quickly and that is normal. It wears out with km and time. Follow the service interval in your manual.

    RECALL QUIZ

    No scrolling up. Answer in your head first.

    1. Name the four strokes in order. Which one makes power?

    Suck (intake), squeeze (compression), bang (power), blow (exhaust). Only the bang pushes the piston. The other three are paid for by the flywheel and the other cylinders.

    2. What turns the up-and-down piston into spinning?

    The connecting rod and crankshaft. The rod pushes on an offset pin on the crank, like your leg on a bicycle pedal.

    3. Why can't engines squeeze the mix as hard as they like?

    Squeezed too hard, the hot mix explodes by itself (knock), which hammers the piston. Octane tells you how well the fuel resists it.

    Spaced review from yesterday (Day 3, Suspension and steering):

    4. You push down on one corner of a car and it bounces three times. What is worn?

    The shock absorber (damper). The spring still holds the car up, but nothing is turning the bounce into heat.

    5. What is toe, and what does too much of it do?

    Toe is the front tyres pointing slightly in or out. Too much drags each tyre sideways as it rolls, so the edges wear out early and you use more fuel.

    HOW IT CONNECTS
    • Day 2, brakes: brakes turn speed into heat. The engine turns heat into speed. Same energy, opposite direction.
    • History: Nikolaus Otto built the first working four-stroke engine in Germany in 1876. Engineers still call this the Otto cycle.
    • Physics: the same squeeze-makes-heat rule is why a cycle pump gets warm at the bottom when you pump hard.
    • Tomorrow, gearbox and clutch: the engine likes to spin 1,500 to 4,000 times a minute. At 100 km/h your wheels turn only about 840 times a minute. Something must match the two.

    • cc (displacement): total space the pistons sweep. 4 cylinders × 42 cm² × 7.15 cm = about 1,197 cc, a "1.2 litre" engine.
    • rpm: crank turns per minute. Idle is about 700 to 900. Each cylinder fires once every 2 turns.
    • Torque: how hard the engine twists. Power: torque × speed. Power in kW = torque (Nm) × rpm ÷ 9,549.
    • TDC / BDC: top dead centre and bottom dead centre, where the piston stops for an instant.
    • Camshaft: turns at half the crank speed, driven by a belt or chain. If a timing belt snaps, many engines get bent valves.
    • Knock: mix explodes by itself. Modern engines have a knock sensor that delays the spark to stop it.
    • Fuel injection: modern engines spray petrol with injectors controlled by a computer. Old engines used a carburettor.
    • 3 vs 4 cylinders: 3 is lighter and cheaper with less friction, but shakes a bit more at idle.
