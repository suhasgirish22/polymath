Why can't your car start in 5th gear?

Today you will mesh gears with your finger, build a 5-speed gearbox and clutch in 3D, and find out why the clutch pedal exists at all.

START WITH A PUZZLE

You are first at a red light in a small manual hatchback. The light turns green. By mistake, the lever is in 5th gear, not 1st. You let the clutch up as usual.

What happens?

Guess first. Even a wrong guess helps you remember the answer.

CHAPTER 1

A small gear turns a big gear

Think of your bicycle on a steep road. You pick the biggest gear at the back. Your legs spin fast, the cycle goes slowly, but the pedals feel easy. On a flat road you pick a small gear. Now each push moves you far, but it feels heavy.

That is the whole secret. Gears swap speed for twist. You cannot get more of both.

Below, the small gear has 12 teeth. It is the engine side. Give the big gear more teeth and watch:

3.5 : 1

The maths is simple. Ratio = big teeth ÷ small teeth. 42 ÷ 12 = 3.5. The small gear must turn 3.5 times to turn the big one once. In return, the big gear twists 3.5 times harder. A long spanner works the same way: more twist, but your hand moves further.

CHAPTER 2

The engine has a happy zone

On Day 4 you saw the engine spin. It has a problem. Below about 1,000 rpm the bangs are too far apart. It shakes and stops. Above about 6,000 rpm a computer cuts the fuel to save it.

But your wheels need everything from 0 to about 1,000 turns a minute. At a signal they are still. At 100 km/h they turn about 850 times a minute.

So the gearbox keeps the engine happy while the wheels do whatever they need. Pick a gear, a speed and a road. Watch the rev needle:

Now solve the puzzle: try 5th gear at 5 km/h

The engine would need to turn only about 140 times a minute. That is far below its happy zone. In 5th, the engine also gives less than one fifth of the push it gives in 1st. So the clutch drags the engine down until it stalls.

CHAPTER 4

The clutch: joining a spinning thing to a still thing

Rub your palms together. Press lightly and they slide. Press hard and they grip and move together. A clutch is two big "palms" made of metal and friction material.

One palm is the flywheel. It always spins with the engine. The other is the clutch plate, joined to the gearbox. A strong spring squeezes them together with about 400 kg.

Press the pedal and the spring is pulled back. The plates part. Now you can stop the car, or change gear, while the engine keeps running. Lift your foot slowly, as at a signal:

Clutch grip
0 Nm
Push on the car, in 1st
0 kg
Heat in the clutch
0 kW

Where does that grip come from? Grip = friction (0.3) × spring force (4,000 N) × average radius (7.5 cm) × 2 faces. That is about 180 Nm. The engine makes at most 113 Nm. So a healthy clutch never slips once your foot is off.

The point where the plates just start to grip is the bite point. While they slip, the engine's work becomes heat. A few seconds is fine. Holding a car on a slope with the clutch for half a minute is not. That is the burnt smell you sometimes notice near flyovers.

Why the gears need it too: when you change gear, the collar inside must catch a gear that spins at a different speed. Pressing the clutch frees the input shaft, and a small brass cone (the synchro) rubs the speeds equal. Without it: you hear a loud grinding crunch.

CHAPTER 5

Cars that change gear for you

More and more Indian cars have two pedals, not three. They still need gears, because the engine still has a happy zone. Something else does the shifting. Tap each type:

TRUE OR FALSE?

Three things people believe

"Resting your left foot lightly on the clutch pedal while driving does no harm."

False. Even light pressure can ease the spring a little, so the plates slip and wear. It also keeps the release bearing working all the time. Rest your foot on the floor or the dead pedal.

"On a slope, hold the car still at the bite point. It saves the brakes."

False. The plates slip the whole time and turn engine work into heat (Chapter 4). Brakes are cheap to fix. A clutch is a big job. Use the foot brake or handbrake, and many new cars have hill-hold.

"Going downhill in neutral saves a lot of petrol."

False for modern cars. Lift off the accelerator while in gear, and the computer stops the petrol fully: the wheels keep the engine turning. In neutral, the engine burns petrol just to idle. You also lose engine braking (Days 2 and 4).

RECALL QUIZ

No scrolling up. Answer in your head first.

1. 1st gear gives you more of what, and less of what?

More twist (push at the wheels), less speed. The engine turns about 15 times for one turn of the wheel in 1st, but only about 3 times in 5th.

2. In a manual gearbox, how does it choose one gear if all the gears always touch?

The gears on the output shaft spin freely. A sliding collar, moved by the fork and lever, locks just one of them to the shaft. Only that pair passes the power.

3. Why does the engine stall if you lift the clutch too fast at a signal?

The wheels are still. Once the clutch grips fully, it tries to stop the engine too. At low rpm the engine cannot twist hard enough to get the car moving, so it is pulled below its happy zone and stops.

Spaced review from yesterday (Day 4, The petrol engine):

4. Only one stroke of four makes power. What keeps the crank turning between bangs?

The heavy flywheel, and the other cylinders. It is the same flywheel the clutch grips today.

5. What is knock, and what does the octane number tell you?

Knock is the squeezed mix exploding by itself before or apart from the spark. Octane tells you how well the petrol resists it. It is not extra energy.

HOW IT CONNECTS
  • Day 4, engine: the flywheel that smooths the bangs is also the clutch's first "palm". The rpm limits you saw there are why gears exist.
  • Physics: a gear is a lever that keeps turning. Twist = force × radius, the idea Archimedes used for levers over 2,000 years ago.
  • Day 3, steering: the differential inside the final drive lets the outer wheel turn faster than the inner wheel on a turn.
  • Tomorrow, EVs and batteries: an electric motor gives full twist from 0 rpm and spins past 10,000 rpm. So most EVs have one fixed gear, about 9 : 1, and no clutch at all.

  • Gear ratio: driven teeth ÷ driving teeth. Above 1 = slower and stronger. Below 1 (overdrive) = faster than the engine.
  • Final drive: one more fixed gear pair before the wheels, usually about 3.5 to 4.5 in small cars. Total = gear ratio × final drive.
  • Synchromesh: brass cone rings that match the speed of the collar and gear before they lock. Almost all car forward gears have it.
  • Reverse: an extra small gear (idler) sits between two gears and flips the direction. Many cars have no synchro on reverse, so stop fully first.
  • Clutch parts: flywheel, clutch plate (friction disc), pressure plate with diaphragm spring, release bearing. Worked by a cable or by fluid.
  • Clutch life: varies a lot with driving. Heavy city traffic and riding the clutch wear it out far sooner.
  • Slipping clutch: engine rpm jumps but the car does not speed up, often in a high gear on a hill. Get it checked.
  • Automatics in India: AMT (cheap, a robot works a normal gearbox), torque converter (smooth, oil coupling), CVT (belt and cones, no steps), DCT (two clutches, very fast shifts).
